For centuries, classical optics has relied on the same fundamental mechanism: bulk refraction. To bend a beam of light, focus an image, or correct for chromatic aberration, optical components require curved glass elements. Achieving high precision often demands multi-lens assemblies—resulting in bulky, heavy camera modules and thick optical setups.

**Flat optics** fundamentally disrupts this paradigm. By replacing curved geometry with a two-dimensional array of subwavelength structures—known as a **metasurface**—we can control phase, amplitude, and polarization locally across an ultra-thin planar surface.

In this post, we will cover:
1. The transition from classical continuous phase accumulation to abrupt phase jumps.
2. The hyperbolic phase profile required for an ideal focusing **metalens**.
3. The physical mechanism of **meta-atoms** (nanopillars and nanofins).
4. How subwavelength phase shifting is modeled using Maxwell solvers.

---

## 1. Bending Light: Phase Accumulation vs. Phase Discontinuity

In a conventional glass lens, light bends because it travels slower inside the optical medium ($v = c/n$). The phase shift $\Delta \phi$ accumulates gradually over a physical thickness $d$:

$$\Delta \phi = k_0 \int n(z) \, dz$$

where $k_0 = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda_0}$ is the free-space wavevector. To create a parabolic wavefront that converges light to a single point, the lens must be physically thicker in the middle and thinner at the edges.

Metasurfaces change the rule: instead of accumulating phase along a long propagation path $d$, we introduce **abrupt, localized phase discontinuities** $\Delta \Phi(x, y)$ at a flat interface. 

According to the **Generalized Snell's Law of Refraction**, an artificial phase gradient $\frac{d\Phi}{dx}$ along the interface steers the incoming wave vector:

$$n_t \sin\theta_t - n_i \sin\theta_i = \frac{1}{k_0} \frac{d\Phi}{dx}$$

By tailoring $\frac{d\Phi}{dx}$ across a 2D plane, a completely flat surface can behave like a converging lens, a beam deflector, or a complex waveplate.

## 2. The Ideal Metalens Phase Profile

To transform an incoming planar wavefront ($\lambda_0$) into a converging spherical wavefront focused at a distance $f$ (the focal length), every point $(x, y)$ on the flat surface must impart a specific spatial phase shift $\Phi(r)$:

$$\Phi(r) = -\frac{2\pi}{\lambda_0} \left( \sqrt{r^2 + f^2} - f \right)$$

where:
* $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ is the radial distance from the optical center.
* $f$ is the focal length.
* $\lambda_0$ is the design wavelength in free space.

Because light phases repeat every $2\pi$ radians, the target continuous profile $\Phi(r)$ is wrapped into the range $[0, 2\pi)$. The challenge is to realize an artificial material that can map any coordinate $(x,y)$ to a precise phase value between $0$ and $2\pi$.

---

## 3. Building Blocks: Meta-Atoms

To achieve local phase control, the surface of a metalens is populated by an array of subwavelength building blocks called **meta-atoms**. These are high-index dielectric nanostructures (such as Titanium Dioxide $\text{TiO}_2$, Silicon $\text{Si}$, or Gallium Nitride $\text{GaN}$) etched onto a low-index substrate (like fused silica).

Meta-atoms achieve phase control primarily through two physical mechanisms:

### 1. Propagation / Waveguide Phase
By varying the cross-sectional geometry (e.g., changing the diameter $D$ of a circular dielectric nanopillar), the effective refractive index $n_{\text{eff}}(D)$ of the guided optical mode changes. 
As light travels through the height $H$ of the pillar, it experiences a controlled phase delay:

$$\Delta \phi = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda_0} \cdot n_{\text{eff}}(D) \cdot H$$

### 2. Geometric (Pancharatnam-Berry) Phase
For circularly polarized light, rotating an anisotropic meta-atom (such as a rectangular nanofin) by an angle $\theta$ relative to the spatial axis imparts a phase shift proportional to the rotation:

$$\Delta \phi = \pm 2\theta$$

This geometric phase relies entirely on spatial orientation, making it frequency-independent across specific broad operational bands.

## 4. Designing a Metalens via Numerical Simulation

To build a functioning metalens, the unit-cell nanostructures must first be rigorously characterized. Because meta-atoms operate at subwavelength scales where scattering and wave interactions are dominant, analytical approximations fall short. 

Engineers use **Finite-Difference Time-Domain (FDTD)** simulations on a discretized **Yee Grid** to solve Maxwell's curl equations inside the nanostructures:

1. **Unit Cell Sweep**: Simulate a single nanopillar surrounded by periodic boundary conditions (PML top/bottom, periodic sides).
2. **Phase Library Generation**: Compute the complex transmission $T(D) \cdot e^{i\phi(D)}$ for various diameters $D$ and heights $H$ to find configurations that cover the full $0 \to 2\pi$ phase range with high transmission efficiency ($|T| \approx 1$).
3. **Full Wavefront Assembly**: Place meta-atoms across the 2D grid matching the target profile $\Phi(r) = -\frac{2\pi}{\lambda_0} \left( \sqrt{r^2 + f^2} - f \right)$.

## Summary

* **Phase Discontinuity**: Metalenses bypass the need for thick, curved optical elements by creating sharp, subwavelength phase shifts at a flat interface.
* **Hyperbolic Profile**: Achieving diffraction-limited focusing requires mapping a hyperbolic phase function $\Phi(r)$ wrapped modulo $2\pi$ across the surface.
* **Meta-Atoms**: High-index dielectric nanopillars act as localized subwavelength waveguides, imparting precise phase shifts based on their geometry or orientation.
* **Computational Electromagnetics**: Maxwell solvers like FDTD on the Yee Grid are essential for building phase libraries and simulating light-matter interactions at the nanoscale.
